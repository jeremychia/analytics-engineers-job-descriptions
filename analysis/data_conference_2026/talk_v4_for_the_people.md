# Let them in, help them up, teach them — v4, for a senior room

Draft 7, 4 November 2026. The deck is `jd_deck_v4.html` (nine slides and one for questions, built from `build/src/slides_v4.js`). Use this version when most of the room hires, leads or mentors. Use v3 (`talk_v3_for_the_people.md`, `jd_deck_v2.html`) when most of the room is looking for work. The thinking behind this version is in `rethink_growing_people.md`; evidence tables and tests stay in `talk_v2_for_the_people.md`.

## The shape

- **The question:** job ads are the field's public record of how it lets people in, helps them up, and who teaches them. What does that record say?
- **The frame:** junior ads ask a lot and promise little. They ask for years and a degree. Few say who will teach the new hire, or give them anything to learn with.
- **The headline:** three in four junior ads don't say who will teach the new hire. Half name neither a teacher nor anything to learn with. All 64 junior ads were read by hand.
- **The one action:** if you mentor, say so in the junior ad. It is in a senior person's control; headcount and recruiter templates are not.
- **The one sentence:** "Someone held the door for each of us, then taught us. Do both for the next one."
- **What the job is:** four duties come up in three ads in four, and working with stakeholders is one of them. SQL is in almost every ad; after SQL, Python and dbt, no tool is in more than four ads in ten. SQL and people are the floor, and the rest is local.
- **The junior gates:** two asks fall on juniors in particular. One junior ad in five asks for two years or more. Six in ten mention a degree, against one in three senior ads.
- **The progression gap:** SQL is in about 95% of ads from mid level up, flat. The business is named in about a third, also flat. On the Reis and Handy podcast, what opens a career path is being curious how the business works. The ads may lag what the next step needs. It is a question for the room, not a charge against the people who write them.
- **What comes next:** the ads can't show a trend. A few already show the first job moving from writing SQL to reviewing what a tool wrote, so the judgement has to be taught.
- **About ten numbers spoken:** 1,200 ads, 3 in 4, 4 in 10, 2–5 years, 1 in 9, 1 in 5, 6 in 10, 3 ads, a third, 94% and 95%, a third again, 17 of 64, half, 19%, 22 and 5. Everything else lives in the notes and the Q&A.
- **Tone:** the ads are the subject, never the people who write them.

## The script (about 6:23 at 130 words a minute, about 830 words; about 6:42 with the pauses)

**[The ad · on screen, typed in]**
"Raise your hand if someone gave you your first job in data. Now keep it up if you've since given someone theirs.

Look at the difference between those two. That difference is what this talk is about.

This is a real ad. It says junior. Then it asks for two to five years. If this were your first ad, would you have got in?

I read about 1,200 job ads for analytics engineers. So I asked three questions. How do we let people in? How do we help them up? And who teaches them?"

**[First · What is the job?]**
"First, what is the job? I sorted the duties in each ad. Four come up in three ads in four: reporting, data modelling, data quality, and working with stakeholders. Working with people is in as many ads as modelling.

The tools are simpler. SQL is in almost every ad. Then Python and dbt. After those, no tool is in more than four ads in ten. So SQL and people are the floor. The rest is local, and each team teaches its own."

**[Let them in · How many doors are there?]**
"Start with the door. In the ads I read, for every junior ad there are nine at senior level or above.

Every senior in this room was once that person. Someone took a chance on a person with no years to show. Nine to one. That's one snapshot, so it can't tell me whether the door is narrowing. I'll come back to that."

**[Let them in · What stands in the way?]**
"And the junior ads that do exist? One in five asks for two years or more. One says junior in its title and asks for two years. One has junior in its web address and asks for three. That's the ask that breaks the label.

Then the degree. Six in ten junior ads mention one. Senior ads, one in three. When there are no years to ask for, the ad asks for a degree instead. Three ads in 1,200 name another route in: a bootcamp, teaching yourself, or a different background."

**[Help them up · What changes at each step?]**
"Once someone is in, how do they move up? That floor stays the same at every level.

What changes is the verb. You can read four of them on screen. As the level goes up, 'support' falls and 'own' rises.

From mid to senior, one verb arrives: mentor. A third of senior ads ask you to mentor, coach or review code. A third of senior jobs expect you to teach."

**[Help them up · What moves you up?]**
"One thing doesn't change: SQL. It's in ninety-four percent of mid-level ads and ninety-five percent of senior ones.

Tristan Handy says there is a career path for people curious how the business works. But only a third of ads name the business, at any level. It isn't a rung.

So how do you move up? The ads don't say. Maybe they lag: they list what the last hire knew, not what the next promotion needs. You tell me if that's honest."

**[Pass it on · Who teaches the juniors?]**
"So who teaches the juniors? I read all sixty-four junior ads by hand. Seventeen tell the new hire who will teach them.

The other forty-seven don't say.

And a learning budget doesn't fill the gap. Half of all junior ads name neither: no one to learn from, nothing to learn with. Junior ads ask a lot. They promise very little.

The best one I found was a bank in New Zealand: 'dedicated time for training and certification, with guidance and mentorship from Senior/Staff Engineers and Chapter Leads.' One sentence. It tells a junior who, and when.

The senior who would teach them is already there. They're just not in the ad. If you mentor, say so in the junior ad.

The ads can't tell me whether the juniors who got in were taught. You can. Come and tell me after."

**[What comes next · What does AI change?]**
"Most of you are thinking about AI. So am I.

These ads can't tell me whether AI is narrowing the junior door. Outside data says to watch it. In the US, Stanford finds employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs is 19% below trend. The authors call that a pattern, not proof.

A few ads already say how the first job is changing. Zego's says it plainly: 'AI tools now handle a lot of the heavy lifting of writing SQL.' The new hire reviews it instead. Twenty-two junior ads ask for AI. Five say who teaches.

Most of us learned to review by writing bad SQL and having someone read it. If the tool writes the first draft, someone still has to teach the judgement. SQL is the first thing the tool does.

In ten years, someone has to review what the AI writes. That person is a junior today."

**[The ad, again]**
"Back to Enhance. It got one line right: 'Strong internships or graduate projects count.' A handful of ads say the same. Keep that. Cut the two to five years. And add the line most junior ads are missing: who will teach them.

Someone held the door for each of us, then taught us. Do both for the next one."

**Retired from the script after review:**
- "Ten seniors asked to mentor for every junior told who." It set a sample estimate against a full count with a different rule, and most of the ratio came from the 9-to-1 mix.
- "Junior ads say 'support', mid-level ads say 'own'." Mid-level ads still say "support" more often than "own".
- "The more senior the ad, the more it asks for AI." Ad length explains most of the climb above junior.
- "Junior ads need the local language more often." 17% against 9% across all ads, but 7% against 4% on English ads alone (not significant). The gap comes from which languages the junior ads are in.
- "Two in three AI-tool asks never mention checking the output." True only within the same sentence (35% mention it). Counting the sentence either side, 58% do.
- "Junior ads state pay least often." Outside the US it is flat (junior 17%, mid 16%, senior 15%, lead 23%). The gap comes from how few junior ads are American.
- "Junior ads ask for AI tools as often as senior ads." True on English ads (33% against 37%, only 43 junior ads), not across all languages (23% against 34%).
- The pay lines. "A quarter give the pay" is driven by US ads. The EU directive does not require pay in the ad, and the reviewer reported that France had not yet written it into law.

## Q&A ready

- **"Isn't one in nine just what you collected?"** Partly. The ads were collected during one person's job search, which leans senior. That is why the script says "in the ads I read".
- **"Do juniors come in as analysts instead?"** Possibly. The corpus is analytics-engineering and BI ads. Analyst roles are a likely door the corpus does not cover.
- **"Doesn't a learning budget count as support?"** It does, and it's counted: 18 junior ads offer a budget, a programme, courses or set time to learn. Most of those still don't say who will teach. Half of junior ads offer neither.
- **"An ad that says nothing doesn't mean nobody mentors."** Agreed. The point is what a junior can see before applying. The mentoring may exist; the ad doesn't say so.
- **"Do ads that name a mentor get better hires?"** The ads can't show outcomes. Ask the room.
- **"Isn't AI why there are fewer junior roles?"** The ads can't show a trend: a few months of ads, collected during one search. Stanford's US data is the best outside signal, and its authors say it is descriptive. What the ads show is the job moving from writing to reviewing.
- **"Isn't a degree a fair proxy when there's no experience?"** Maybe. But only 10 of the 37 say "or equivalent experience", or words like it, anywhere in the ad. Saying that projects count shows the other way in.
- **"So are ads dishonest about what gets you promoted?"** I can't say. Ads are written from templates and from the last hire, and they describe the job, not the promotion. The ads can't show what actually gets people promoted. Ask the room.
- **"Isn't business knowledge hard to put in an ad?"** Yes, and that may be the point. SQL is easy to list, so it gets listed. The things that move people up are harder to write down.
- **"Isn't this about headcount, not wording?"** Headcount is often not ours to decide. The mentor line in the ad is.
- **"What should my ad offer?" or "What can I ask for?"** Go to slide 9. Each region has its own norm, as a % of ads that list each benefit. A learning budget is listed in 18% of Nordic ads and 43% of CEE ads. Read the ads around you as the norm.
- **"Your duty chart doesn't show mentoring."** It is 15% of all ads, counted on the responsibility bullets only: 23% of senior and 43% of lead ads. The hand read of 50 senior ads (a third) reads the whole ad, so it finds more.
- **Pay transparency:**
  - **Outside the US:** about one ad in six states pay.
  - **The EU directive:** it gives applicants the right to the pay range before the interview, not necessarily in the ad.
  - **France:** check its transposition status before the talk.
- **Stack:** at mid level, dbt ads without Power BI ask people to define, own or lead 25% of the time; Power BI ads without dbt, 5%. A different stack offers a different room to grow.
