# Read the ad, not the adjectives — a talk for the people, v3

Draft 4, 27 October 2026. The stage deck is `jd_deck_v2.html` (eight slides, built from `build/src/slides_v2.js`). The 10-minute version uses `jd_deck.html`, which adds AI and benefits. Evidence tables, statistical tests, outside sources, claims to cut and Q&A stay in `talk_v2_for_the_people.md`.

## The shape

- **The one sentence:** "A job ad says one thing and often means another, and no two ads are alike. So read what each one asks, and answer with your own story." Spoken short: "Read what it asks. Then answer with your own story."
- **Finish at 4:30, not 5:00.** The last 30 seconds absorb a late start, a laugh or a slow click. The script is written at about 130 words a minute; if it runs long, cut a section rather than speed up.
- **One theme, said three times:** read what the ad asks, then answer with your own story. Plant it in the opening, return to it in the juniors section, land it in the close.
- **Six questions, not eight.** Each has one number and one real ad line. Everything else is on the slide or in Q&A.
- **Walk through the work.** Where the data surprised you, say what you looked for first, then what the ads showed. Say only expectations you really held.
- **One plain word per idea.** Say "asks for two years or more", never "gate": senior ads carry more of those asks, so only the years break the junior label. "The core" is named before it is used. "Strategic" becomes "define, own or lead". "Classifier" never reaches the spoken script.

## The script (4:52, about 630 words)

**[The ad · on screen, typed in]**
`Enhance is hiring a Junior Analytics Engineer in Dubai to help turn our data into insights…`
`2-5 years in analytics, BI or data engineering. Strong internships or graduate projects count.` *(Enhance, Dubai)*

"I was looking for my next job. Not a junior one. But on the way, I kept running into ads like this. It says junior. Then it asks for two to five years.

I got curious. What is it like to read this when you're starting out? So I kept reading. About 1,200 ads for analytics engineers, at every level. Each time I asked one question: when the ad says this, what does it mean? What I learned is to read what an ad asks, not what it calls itself."

**[Question 1 · Does the title tell me the job?]**
"First, the title. 358 different titles on 908 ads. The role was first written up almost eight years ago. There's still no shared definition.

So I looked for a template. People copy what works, so I expected ads to borrow from each other. They mostly don't. Two ads from different companies are about four percent alike. Real copying happens inside one company, like Amazon's teams. Every ad asks for its own mix. Hold on to that. It's good news, and I'll come back to it."

**[Question 2 · If the title doesn't tell me the job, what does?]**
"So what does tell you the job? The level matters most. But take two ads at the same level, and look at the stack. Mid-level ads that name dbt and not Power BI ask you to define, own or lead 25% of the time. Power BI ads without dbt: 5%.

Read the first duty. January: 'Own the gold layer.' Lenovo: 'supporting Lenovo's Finance team.' That isn't a ranking of tools. It's a choice. Want to own the model? Look for that verb. Love building dashboards? Those ads are full of them, and that's a real job too."

**[Question 3 · If I'm starting out, is there a door?]**
"Now juniors, where this started. This is the one I worry about. About one ad in twenty is for a junior. So is the ad I opened with the rule?

One junior ad in five asks for two years or more. That's the ask that breaks the label. Four in five don't: almost half the junior ads state no years at all, and the others ask for a year or less. And a handful say what Enhance said: internships or projects count.

So if you have the basics, apply. Lead with the project. That project is your story."

**[Question 4 · How do I move up?]**
"Once you're in, how do you move up? The core never changes. SQL, a warehouse, testing your data, working with people. Ads ask for it at every level.

What changes is the verb. As the level goes up, 'support' falls and 'own' rises. Senior ads add setting the standards. Dex: 'Set the standards for data quality.' No junior ad asks you to define, own or lead. Eight in ten lead ads do.

So practise the next verb on the work you already do."

**[Question 5 · What doesn't the ad tell me?]**
"Then I turned the question around. What doesn't the ad tell you? A quarter of ads give the pay, and most of those are American. Fewer than one in ten say how you'll be interviewed, or who you'll report to. Two in a hundred say how big the team is. The ad asks a lot. It rarely answers.

So if it's not in the ad, ask on the first call."

**[Question 6 · What do I do with this?]**
"So what do you do with 1,200 ads? Six in ten have a mix of tools no other ad has. Usually it's one tool apart. The same core, and a different tail.

If you hire: read your own ad again, and answer those questions. The pay, the steps, the team.

If you want the next level: practise the next verb.

If you're starting out: the core is the same everywhere, and no two ads are alike. So you can be the niche. Every brilliant analytics engineer I know came from somewhere different. That's your story. Pitch it."

**[The ad, again]**
"That ad said junior, and asked for two to five years. Then it said projects count. Read what it asks. Then answer with your own story."

## Question 5: what each missing item changes

The slide measures what the ad text says. It does not measure what a candidate can find elsewhere, or what happens on a call. Use these notes to decide what stays on the slide and what the spoken line says.

- **Interview steps (9% of ads):** many companies describe their hiring process once, on a careers or "how we hire" page, not in each ad. The corpus holds the ad text only, so 9% understates what a candidate can find. It is the cheapest gap to close, and the least surprising. Say "check the careers page first", or drop it from the spoken line.
- **Pay (25%):** the ads cannot show whether a recruiter brings it up on a call. Recruiters often ask for your expectation first, so ask for the range early, before the first interview. In the EU, the pay transparency directive (2023/970, article 5) gives applicants the right to the starting pay or its range before the interview, and bars questions about pay history. Few countries had written it into law by the 7 June 2026 deadline, so say "is becoming a right", not "is a right".
- **Who you report to (7%):** this tells you whether you sit in a data or engineering team, or inside a business team such as finance or marketing. It decides who reviews your work and who judges when you are ready for the next verb (question 4). For a junior, it decides whether anyone above you knows the craft.
- **How big the team is (2%):** this tells you whether you are the only analytics engineer or one of several. Alone, the scope is wide and nobody reviews your models. In a team, the scope is narrower and there is someone to learn from. For a junior, this is the most important question on the slide.
- **Years (54%) and remote or office (60%):** most ads state these, so they are the comparison on the slide, not the advice.

The last two items carry the story. They join question 5 to the juniors section: the question a junior should ask on the first call is "who will I learn from?" Neither can be tested from the ads; say them as advice, not as findings.

- [ ] Change the spoken pay line to "ask for the range before the first interview".
- [ ] Replace "ask on the first call" with the one question that matters most: "who will I learn from?"

## Slides

- **Each headline states the finding.** The question sits above it as a small label.
- **Footers are plain.** Each one gives the sample and what the measure means, without test names. The full test detail stays in the `jd_deck.html` footers and the v2 evidence tables.
- **The adjectives line is for Q&A only.** It comes from the earlier 942-ad pass and has no chart: "I tested 'fast-paced', 'self-starter' and 'wear many hats'. They told me nothing about the job."

## Rehearsal

- [ ] Memorise three things word for word: the first two sentences, and "Read what it asks. Then answer with your own story." Speak the middle from the running order, not from memory.
- [ ] Do at least five full runs out loud, standing, with a timer that starts on your first word.
- [ ] Runs 1–2: cut anything that pushes the total past 4:45.
- [ ] Runs 3–4: mark where you stall or leave the script, and fix those lines.
- [ ] Record one run. Listen for pace, filler words and whether the close lands.
- [ ] Run it once in front of colleagues. Note where they look lost or check their phones.
- [ ] Check the time at 2:30 on every run. It should land within 15 seconds of the start of the juniors slide.
- [ ] Run the 3-minute emergency version once.
- [ ] Stop when three runs in a row finish under 4:45.
- [ ] Mark three pauses: after "two to five years", after "four percent alike", after "That's your story."

## On the day

- [ ] Open `jd_deck_v2.html` full-screen on the first slide before your slot, with short mode off.
- [ ] Start speaking on the ad. No introduction, no agenda and no "I'll be quick."
- [ ] Look at the clock once, at 2:30, and use the cut points if you are behind.
- [ ] If you lose a section, skip to the next question. Nobody else knows the script.
- [ ] After the last line, pause for two seconds and stop. The deck ends on the ad, not on a "thank you" or "questions" slide.
- [ ] If there are questions, bridge into them: "I cut AI and benefits for time. Happy to go there."
