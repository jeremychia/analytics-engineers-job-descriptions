## 0b. The why and the how — say both

How you deliver and what drives you are two separate things. Your assessments say you don't perform warmth. That is about delivery. It says nothing about the motive underneath, and being undemonstrative sits perfectly well alongside being driven by fairness — in your case the record says it does.

**The why: closing information gaps.** Your own GitHub README, written long before any of this, says you build data products that "reduce information asymmetry." Three years teaching Python at ReDI School, to migrants and people shut out of tech. A nonprofit board seat, running its finances, mentoring thirty interns. That is not a register you're borrowing. It's the most consistent thing in your file.

And the job market is the most asymmetric market most people ever participate in. The employer knows exactly what they mean; the applicant is guessing from words that turn out to mean 98 different things. **So this project is the purest version of a thing you wrote down years ago.** That connection is the strongest single idea available to you today. One line carries it:

> I've been building things to close information gaps for years. This is just the biggest one I've found.

**The how: leaving things that work without you.** Read your star stories end to end and they all say the same thing. The testing guide, and then Arabella and Quirine wrote their own without you. The works council election that became the model other offices used. The intern hiring process — the hire didn't work out, the template outlived it. The workshops that set the next quarter's priorities. The data catalogue that moved the guild's standards.

That's in your motivations verbatim: "enabling impact at scale through others' capability, not just my own output."

**Neither half works alone.** Advocacy with no method is a speech. A method with no reason behind it is just engineering. Say the why when they ask what drives you, show the how when they ask what you did — and let the open dataset, the published mistakes, and "go and disagree with me" be the proof that you mean the first one.

## 3. How "personable and humble" actually happens

Your assessments read Blue 71 / Red 75 / **Yellow 20**. High analysis, high drive, very little natural expressiveness. ISTP. First impression: serious, direct, a bit intense. Reality: you care a lot and show it through reliability ([personality.md](../../jeremychia/resume/profile/personality.md)).

In a room, that gap closes over an hour. On camera it doesn't. A 30-second clip gives you no time to earn the benefit of the doubt. So six things, in order of how much they'll move it.

**1. Change who the number is for. Don't soften it.** This is the whole trick. Hedging reads as uncertainty, not modesty — and uncertainty is already your flagged weakness. So keep the number and move the beneficiary. Not "74% of early-stage roles build from scratch" but "if you want to build something, that's a line you can believe." Humility is about who you're serving. It isn't about doubting yourself out loud.

**2. Start with the confusion, not the conclusion.** You built this because you were job hunting and couldn't tell the ads apart. That's the most human fact in the project. Lead with it.

**3. Admit one specific thing per answer.** "I've a lot to learn" is noise. Specific lands: *I called it validated when it wasn't. A bug dropped three fields. My dashboard had too much in it and people told me so.* One per answer. Then move on. Dwelling reads as fishing.

**4. Use names.** "Arabella and Quirine wrote their own from the guide" beats "two engineers did." It costs nothing and shows you notice people.

This matters more today, because what people come to you for is *introductions*. A connector who talks in categories — "stakeholders," "practitioners," "the community" — undercuts his own claim. Your notes say you're not the social connector, and that's true of rooms. But you connect from substance: you hear what someone's stuck on, you remember it, you know who fixed it last month. Say it in names or it won't land.

**5. Warmth goes in the first two seconds.** Look at Bolaji, not the lens. Start up, not flat. Then be as direct as you like. Directness isn't the problem. A cold open is.

**6. Slow down about 15%.** Under pressure you speed up and clip the ends of sentences. The last three words carry the full stop an editor needs. Land them.

**Two things to resist.** If Bolaji gets a finding slightly wrong, don't correct him. Add to it: *"Yes — and the bit I'd add is…"* Only correct a number, and do it lightly. And a retake is normal production, not failure. Don't redo an answer that was fine.

One note: long social stretches drain you, and there's an unscripted walk to the studio. Fifteen minutes on camera is fine. Don't spend the walk rehearsing. Spend it talking to Bruno.

## 6. If they go off script

**"Isn't this just criticising dbt's survey?"** — likely, and it's Bolaji's job to care.
No. Say it warmly, not defensively. A survey measures what people say about their work. Job ads measure what employers will put in writing, with hiring costs attached. Two instruments, same territory. Where they agree, the finding is much stronger than either alone — governance and trust show up in both, separately. That agreement is the useful part.

**"How do you know your classifications are right?"**
You don't yet. Say that plainly — it's your best humility moment. It's an AI model with consistency checks, and consistency isn't accuracy. Human checking is the first job of the next six months, not the last. Then ask on camera: two or three people, an hour each, reading the same 10–15 ads against the written rules. Asking for help in public fits exactly the impression you want.

**"Biggest limitation?"**
Geography, and say it first. About three-quarters European, because that's where you were looking. Asia is at 109 ads now, enough to compare properly. North America is basically one city.

**"How does this help dbt?"** — likely, since product feedback is on their list.
Two ways. It's an instrument they don't have: the survey asks people what they want, the corpus reads what employers will pay for. And one concrete thing — 63% of ads name dbt, but manager-written ads name it 69% of the time against 35% for recruiter-written. That gap is about who wrote the ad, not about adoption. Worth knowing before anyone reads the market off job boards.

**If Bolaji raises the vendor question himself.**
Then be blunter, and it'll land better than hedging. The survey is published by the company selling the tool the survey validates. Worth naming. Then go straight to the generous half: that's exactly why an independent instrument helps, the two agree on governance and trust, and agreement from two directions is far stronger than either. In that order. Criticism without the agreement sounds like an attack. Agreement alone sounds like dodging.

## 7. The cringe risks, named

**The biggest one is structural, not any single line.** Nearly every answer here ends on a crafted closing sentence. Eleven of those in a row stops sounding like a conversation and starts sounding like a keynote. So let three of them just stop, flat, with nothing on the end. Unevenness is what makes the good lines sound unplanned rather than queued up.

The three to flatten, specifically:

- **What makes the community valuable** — stop after *"we tried that and it cost us three weeks."* Cut the expensive-knowledge line.
- **Where is the industry headed** — if you've already made the definition point in the Big Project, stop after the 61%/72% numbers and skip the last paragraph. Only keep it if the Big Project got cut short.
- **The practitioners backup** — *"It looked real. It looked obvious. It was sample size."* Already flat. Leave it.

Keep the warm-up's last line. It's disarming rather than clever, which is a different thing.

**Say the advocacy point once.** The Champion answer, the language-gate point, and closing option 2 are all the same argument. All three is belabouring. Pick the one place it lands hardest — probably the language-gate answer, because it has numbers and a personal fact behind it — and let the other two stay light.

**Same with "the work is agreed, the power isn't."** Say it once, in the Big Project answer. Don't bring it back later as a callback. Callbacks are a stage technique and they read as one.

**Lines I'd watch as you say them:**

| Line | Why it could tip | What saves it |
|---|---|---|
| "Finished projects don't need a community" | Aphorism. Sounds rehearsed if you land it. | Throw it away. No pause, no emphasis. |
| The whole Champion answer | Claiming to speak for ordinary practitioners is a status move if the angle slips. | "I was that person," never "I care about those people." |
| "That second one is what I'm proudest of" | Mild self-praise. | It's pride in something you did *for* others, so it survives. Say it and move straight on. |
| "You're probably more qualified than you think" | Motivational-poster register. | The admission right after — "I nearly didn't apply" — earns it. Don't cut that. |
| "I'm an immigrant" → "people like me" | Speaking on behalf of a group. | Stay on your own case. One person, one story. |
| The 61% vs 72% contrast | Can read as scoring a point off their survey. | Frame as two instruments, and sound interested rather than triumphant. |

**One general test.** If a sentence makes you sound good, it should be about something you did. If it makes you sound wise, cut it. Insight comes from the findings — 98 patterns, 8%, 38% language gates. Those are interesting on their own. You don't need to add the significance out loud; Bolaji can do that in the edit.

## 8. Don't

- Don't soften a checked number to seem modest. It reads as uncertainty. Move the beneficiary instead.
- Don't say "chi-square" or "p-value." Say "the strongest pattern in the data" and point at GitHub.
- Don't explain all ten dimensions. Name two and move on.
- Don't tell the Big Project story in the order you did it. Confusion, the real question, what you found.
- Don't run self-deprecation past one sentence. One specific admission, then move.
- Don't narrate whether an answer went well. Land it and wait.
- Don't raise the management-fashion argument — vendor-funded survey validating vendor tooling. It's sound and it stays in the write-up. Unprompted, in their studio, it's the one thing that could make this footage hard to use. If he raises it, §6.
- Don't finish Bruno's sentences. Build on him instead.
